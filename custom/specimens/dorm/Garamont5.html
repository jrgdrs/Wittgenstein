Claude Garamont
Printing Isn't Enough

Only in hindsight do I realize that Master Augereau, in everything he told me, always kept my young age in mind. He was careful about what he shared with me regarding the books he read — or rather, his feelings while reading them — and what he held back. At the same time, he encouraged me to read, and he often lent me books he had already finished himself.

But I have to admit: in the early days of my apprenticeship, books interested me more as objects — from a technical standpoint — than for their content. I loved running errands for my master. An apprentice was expected to have the quickest feet in the shop, and it thrilled me to pass all the bookstores along the way. They sold what would soon be the product of my own work, and the simple fact that they existed fascinated me enormously. I would have to get to know them. But I didn't pay much attention to what they actually sold.

Of course, I couldn't know them all — not those on the streets leading down to the Seine, nor those near the churches of Saint-Yves, Saint-Mathurin, Saint-Étienne, or Saint-Hilaire, nor those around the schools or our own shop's sign. In the university quarter, there was practically no building that didn't house a printing press or a bookstore.

My journey of discovery began in our immediate neighborhood. To the right of our house, on the Saint-Hilaire side, stood Thibault Charron's bookshop in the house called “At the Sign of Saint Claude.” To the left hung the signs “At the Basel Coat of Arms,” “At the Green Tree,” “At the Flying Heart,“ “At the Image of Saint Christopher,” “At the Deer Horn,“ “At the Samaritan Woman,” and many others.

Behind the Rue Saint-Jean-de-Beauvais lay the Rue des Carmes, where my father owned a house — one of the few without a print shop, though that street was crawling with booksellers too. “At the Good Heart,” “At the Red House,” “At the Pinecone,“ “At the Golden Lion Cubs,” “At the Silver Griffin” ...

The center of it all, however, was and still is the Grand-Rue Saint-Jacques, where there were not just one but several booksellers per building. I never counted them, but I estimate there were nine or ten dozen printer-booksellers on that street alone back then. “At the Lattice,” “At the Unicorn,” “At the White Rose with the Crown,” run by the Le Noir family; in the house “At the Salamander” worked the great Josse Bade, or Badius; then “At the Blooming Lily,” “At the Golden Sun,” “At the Coat of Arms of Burgundy,” “Brittany,” “Cologne,“ “France,” “At the Green Oak,” “At the Pelican” — there's no point going on, I couldn't list them all anyway. On the bridges you'd find mainly sellers: “At the White Rose” on the Pont Saint-Michel, “At the Wild Man” on the Pont au Change — a bookshop where you could buy almost anything in print, as had been the case for decades. I rarely had business at the Palais; they almost never sent me there. But even there, every pillar had one — or several — booksellers.

The only places I didn't like were the execution sites, which lined my routes everywhere. Iron wheels, stone gallows, and pillories could be found all over Paris: at the Place de Grève, near the Halles, at the Croix-du-Trahoir, at the pig market, in Montfaucon, at the Place-aux-Chats, at the Porte Saint-Denis, and especially close to us, at the Porte Saint-Jacques. I can't even count them all. I didn't like passing those places, so with the innocence of a child I would simply close my eyes and try to think about something else. I figured they had nothing to do with me. Now, having watched so many innocent people die there — including friends of mine — I can only think back on them with dread.

From my errands, which I always stretched out as long as I could without risking a scolding, and which I varied from time to time, I always returned with a head full of images, colors, and sounds. And unlike some of the fellow apprentices I came across on my wanderings, I was always glad to come back. Master Antoine had passed his love of the trade on to me.

Strangely enough, it was through typography that I discovered what passion burned inside him.

One evening, after I had retreated to my little nook, the lights were out and the household — I thought — was settling in for the night. That was when Master Estienne and Master Antoine entered the room next door, which was the proofreaders' room and doubtless the best place in the house for a serious conversation. I quickly realized that Robert — the Estienne son who had chosen the printing trade — was with them. As I listened (entirely against my will, since their voices were loud enough to carry through to me no matter how hard I pressed my hands over my ears trying to sleep), I understood that Master Antoine found the books Master Estienne printed boring. At the Estiennes', people spoke Latin as often as possible. Mistress Guyone, Master Henri's wife, spoke it as fluently as her husband; the children had learned it practically from the cradle, and even the servants used it — sometimes among themselves. But this particular conversation was happening in French, for once.

“We never get away from the endless theological annotations and the inevitable Latin commentaries on Aristotle.”

“But what are you saying? Those sell. Very well, in fact.”

“That's true. But I wouldn't mind if we printed things other than scholarly treatises on mathematics and theology.”

“And what exactly do you mean by 'other things,' my dear friend?”

“Books people will actually remember!”

“So you're saying that books used to train doctors, theologians, lawyers, and physicians aren't worth remembering?”

“You know that's not the kind of remembering I mean. I'm talking about truly immortal books — like the works of Aristotle himself.”

“Didn't you just say you were tired of Aristotle?”

“I revere Aristotle. What I'm tired of are the useless and often idiotic commentaries on Aristotle — they seem designed to obscure his texts rather than illuminate them. But there's more than just Aristotle. Haven't you seen the books arriving from Venice at the Pont au Change? Aristophanes, Hesiod, Homer, Virgil... And then there are the great Italian poets who wrote in the vernacular: Dante with his magnificent Divine Comedy, Petrarch and his poems, Boccaccio and his stories. We produce magnificent books, with admirable care, but we're working with our presses instead of our heads.”

“Boccaccio's stories, Father...”

“Please be quiet. I told you to go to bed.”

“Now isn't the time to send this boy to bed, with all due respect. He's our reader of tomorrow — we'll need to educate him and get him interested in what gets printed.”

“Fine. In that case, go ahead and bring in the apprentices while you're at it.”

“I suspect Claude is already listening — he's right on the other side of that wall. Aren't you, Claude?”

I quickly pulled my pants on over my shirt and went out, disheveled and barefoot.

“So now I'm being flooded with youth,” Master Estienne said with a sarcastic edge. “Do you have something to say, Claude?”

“N... No.”

I felt like I was letting Master Antoine down — my mind had gone blank. But Master Antoine was perfectly capable of holding his own; he didn't need me.

“Why don't we print anything in the vernacular, anyway?”

“My dear friend, nobody's asking for it, and besides, the faculty wouldn't exactly welcome it.”

“You know very well what Jacques Lefèvre d'Étaples is doing over at the Abbey of Saint-Germain! He's translating the Bible. Just yesterday he was here and brought us his Commentary on the Epistles of Paul, which I'm preparing for the press, and he told us how happy he was at the thought of Scripture soon being accessible to everyone. Yes, he's translating the Bible into French, drawing on the most reliable sources. He has freed it from the distortions that centuries of scribes layered onto the original texts. The New World has been discovered — surely we can also discover what the sacred texts actually say. Then we can be done with chasing four different senses of Scripture.”

“Where did you get such a foolish idea? 'Done with the fourfold sense'!”

“From Lefèvre himself, and from his writings. It's right there in his Commentary on the Epistles of Paul. For Lefèvre, only the literal meaning counts — the one that opens up the deep spiritual sense of the text. Who could possibly object to that?”

“The entire Sorbonne, with a few exceptions! And—”

“I go to Mass every Sunday like every good Christian, and Sunday after Sunday I have to sit and watch these blockheads twist Scripture every which way until it fits whatever they want it to say. Soon enough they'll have to answer to their flocks for what they've been telling them.” He laughed without any warmth. “I understand that unsettles them a little — they're not used to it.”

“You're saying outrageous things. I'll stick to printing the books people ask me to print.”

Master Antoine burst out laughing — and there was, in fact, something to laugh about. Master Henri was a great scholar with vast knowledge, and before deciding to print a book, he would discuss it at length with the author, or with the commentator if it was a classic. But right now he was angry, and he continued as if Master Antoine had said nothing at all.

“It's not my place to make choices about content. And quite a few scholars have honored me with their friendship because of that stance: the Briçonnets, the du Bellays, Guillaume Budé...”

“I'm not saying anything against your work, Master. God forbid. Or against any of those learned gentlemen, of course. Who in Paris prints more beautiful books than you? No one, I'd dare say — with the occasional exception of Josse Badius, perhaps. And things can absolutely continue that way. But alongside all that, there's room to do something more.”

“Like what, for example?”

“For starters, we should use Roman type of the finest quality.”

“There it is. Are you talking about type or about content?”

“The two go together. New subject matter needs a new form. And since we're on the topic, we could also create Greek type.”

“And who would do that?”

“I would. Who else?”

“That requires specialized expertise...”

“May I remind you, Master Estienne, that I learned Greek as a young child, completed a long apprenticeship as a goldsmith, and ever since I've been here with you I've been cutting type that you use every single day. I don't particularly like it anymore, I should add. I want to do better. You've never had cause to complain about my work, as far as I know. I wanted to become a printer — and I did. But I started out as a theologian, and only then became a type-cutter and type-caster.”

Master Estienne looked at him thoughtfully.

“You never do anything quite the way everyone else does, Antoine. No — don't argue. You simply approach things differently, without any bad intention, without even thinking about it. You don't try to stand out from others; you just give yourself completely to your work. And yet you're recognizable. There are people like that, and you're one of them. So what exactly are you proposing?”

“That we go looking for new texts.”

“What kind of new texts?”

Master Antoine picked up the book he had been absently playing with throughout the conversation.

“A book like this one. Unfortunately, someone got there before us — it's already been published. So we won't be the ones getting rich off it.”

Master Estienne touched it as if it were a live coal. He opened it.

“Encomium Moriae. In Praise of Folly. Just what we needed.”

“Read it, Master. You'll be amazed.”

“I've already read it,” Robert put in. “It's brilliant.”

“What's that to me? And anyway — who gave you this book? I'd wager it was you.” Master Estienne pointed an accusing finger at Master Antoine.

“Certainly not. My apprentice is my witness that I never try to indoctrinate anyone. In Praise of Folly is on everyone's lips right now — that's simply the truth.”

“I read it at the Palais, at Master La Garde's. His wife lets me read books sometimes.”

Henri Estienne looked at his son as if he were a rare species.

“You never stop surprising me, my son. At six he was writing Latin essays; at eight he's reading new releases.”

“He'd like to print them too,“ Robert said with a pointed tone.

“Look at this little wise guy! Enough, young man — I don't want to hear another word from you.”

He turned back to Master Antoine.

“I've been thinking.” That was one of Master Estienne's particular talents: he could hold a conversation while simultaneously working through the hardest problems with the other half of his mind. “For a long time I've been planning a trip to Venice and Padua in search of books worth printing. But I've come to see that I can't leave. Go in my place. I'll write to Aldo Manuzio to announce your arrival and ask him to teach you his style of type-cutting. Monsignor Briçonnet spoke of it with great admiration just a few days ago. And when you're back, we'll see where things stand.”

“Venice?” Master Antoine was speechless. He had been prepared for anything — just not that. “For how long?”

“A year should be enough to see everything, learn something, and find fine texts worth publishing.”

Master Antoine looked at him for a long moment without saying anything.

“Are you sure you're not just trying to get rid of me?” he finally said with a half-smile.

“No, my friend — because now that we've bought another press, I'll have to hire someone to fill in for you, and that's a risk I'd rather not take. But you're probably right. One needs to renew oneself from time to time. I have the greatest confidence in you. Go to Venice. And if Master Garamont agrees to let his son travel, take Claude with you.”

Me?”

“Yes, you. Is there another Claude Garamont in this room?”

Venice! They said it was the most beautiful city in the world. They said the greatest scholars of all could be found there. They said... They said... I tried to remember everything I knew about Venice, but I had never paid much attention to it — not even in my wildest dreams had I imagined actually going there one day.

“Are you listening to me?”

“Um...”

“Honestly, you just can't count on young people anymore. No brain.”

“Forgive me, Master Estienne, I'm listening very carefully.”

“Tomorrow you'll go to your father and talk to him. Actually — no, tell him to come see me instead. And don't say anything else to him. Judging by your expression, you'd be willing to go?”

“Oh, yes.”

That made both masters laugh. Robert didn't laugh. He gave me a sideways look, and as if it were written on his face, I knew exactly what he was thinking: what would it take to get to Venice? At his age, he clearly didn't stand a chance.

“All right then, everyone to bed. We'll sort out the rest tomorrow. Let's all sleep on it.”

To give weight to his words, he picked up the only candle and headed resolutely toward the door. In the half-dark, Master Antoine smiled and winked at me; without saying a word, he left too.

Robert hung back a little. I was heading back to my nook, but in the darkness he grabbed my sleeve.

“Do you see any way I could travel with you?”

“Honestly? Not the slightest. They'll say you're too young. Then again, we're not leaving tomorrow. By the time this all gets off the ground, you might have grown a bit.”

I heard him sigh.

“What's the point of being 'this exceptional child,' as everyone keeps saying, if I still have to act like a perfectly ordinary one?”

I had no idea what to say to that. Into the silence, the door suddenly flew open and Master Estienne appeared, candle in hand.

“What are you still doing here, Robert? It's well past time you were in bed, at your age.”

Robert drew himself up to his full height — at that point he barely reached my shoulder — walked to the door, and whispered so only I could hear: “What did I tell you?”

The next day, of course, nothing unusual happened. It was almost as if the evening's conversation had never taken place. Master Antoine didn't look up from his work; the Commentary on the Epistles of Paul had his complete attention. I went about my errands. I wouldn't have dared to bring up the previous evening myself.

Only when I ran into Robert in a gloomy corner of the shop did I venture a single question:

“That Praise of Folly — did you really read it?”

“Halfway through. It's brilliant.”

“Was it really at the Palais?”

“Yes. If you ask Mistress La Garde politely and wash your hands before touching the book, she'll definitely let you read it. She's very nice.”

“I'll give it a try.”

I wasn't entirely sure about that.

But Mistress La Garde — a round, very lively woman — was in a good mood. “Let me see your hands,” she said that following Saturday, when I slipped out of the workshop after the workday ended. That's what it means to be well-informed: I held out two hands that — which was rare enough — showed no sign of coming from a print shop. They were a genuinely clean pink; I had scrubbed hard for that.

So I was allowed to sit down and leaf through the precious work. I have to admit I was initially disappointed. I had been expecting a fiery polemic, but this was actually a perfectly respectable book, despite its irony. The author, Desiderius Erasmus of Rotterdam — a name I was hearing for the first time that day — teaches us that Folly is mistress of everything and everyone, not Wisdom. The wise man retreats to the books of the ancients and learns nothing but how to shuffle words around; the fool dives straight into things and wrestles with them, and in doing so acquires what I call true wisdom.

That reminded me of the conversation between my two masters about Scripture.

Reading an entire book in one evening was impossible; I had to settle for skimming and browsing. But even so, I could tell that this was a work fundamentally different from anything we dealt with day to day.

I had told my father that Master Estienne had a personal proposal to make to him, and I believe they met — but we all just kept on working as if nothing had happened. No one spoke to me about a trip to Venice anymore, not at the Estiennes' and not at home. From experience, I knew it was pointless to pester my father with questions. I didn't dare ask Master Estienne, and I didn't ask Robert either, though we talked privately on a regular basis.

Then one day, Master Antoine asked me to accompany him to Master Jacques Lefèvre's for proofreading that would go faster with two people. Since Master Lefèvre lived at the Abbey of Saint-Germain, there was a good stretch of road ahead of us, and I would have plenty of time to raise the subject. As it turned out, I didn't need to bring it up at all.

“Can you ride a horse?” Master Antoine asked.

“Not very well. I've never had the chance to do much long-distance traveling.”

“And can you fight?”

“What do you mean by that?”

“Can you handle a blade? A dagger?”

“Not like others can. I never really learned.”

“You'll need to start, if you want to travel safely.”

“Do I have enough time before we leave?” That was my most pressing question — asked without sounding directly like one.

“Yes, you have some time. After endless back-and-forth that I'm honestly tired of by now, Master Estienne finally decided we should travel to Venice by a roundabout route. Your father seems to be on board. You weren't supposed to know yet — why, I don't know — which is exactly why I'm telling you, and asking you to keep it to yourself.”

I wanted to thank him for the relief I felt, but he pushed on without giving me the chance.

“First we'll travel to Basel. One of the proofreaders from the shop, Beat Bildt, who left before you arrived, is now working for a respected printer there — Johannes Froben, or Frobenius. He might be able to help us find books. From Basel we'll head on to Venice, which makes the trip a bit longer but also more interesting. I've always wanted to see the Rhine — they say it's even wider than the Seine.”

A deep sigh.

“If it were up to me, we would have left long ago. But in the end it's Master Estienne who's paying for the trip, and when he insists on managing every last detail himself, I'd rather let him. If I had pushed, it would only have annoyed him.”

We walked in silence for a while. The Porte Saint-Germain was already behind us and we were approaching the Abbey.

“How old are you?” Master Antoine suddenly asked.

“How... um... I'm almost fourteen.”

“And what do you think about theology?”

That truly embarrassed me. I didn't think anything about theology; up to that point, I had never been interested in it.

“I... don't know what to make of it, Master. I see people arguing over words that, to my mind, aren't worth the time it takes to build learned arguments around them.”

He sighed.

“You know what? You're an innocent. You're sensible.”

“Speaking of which, I wanted to ask you something.”

“Go ahead.”

“What exactly is the fourfold sense of Scripture? It keeps coming up, but I've never understood it...”

That made him laugh.

“That cuts right to the heart of it, my dear Claude. According to the theologians at the Sorbonne, Scripture has four separate meanings — which only the most highly educated can truly grasp, and only after years of study: the literal sense, the allegorical sense, the tropological sense, and the anagogical sense.”

“Tropological?”

“You could also say symbolic. But scholars say tropological. Always prefer the learned term — that's the rule of those gentlemen — otherwise people might understand too easily. The difference between allegorical, tropological, and anagogical is rather small, isn't it? Which suits those scholars just fine: it gives them work to do, something to argue about, and above all, the freedom to say about the sacred texts exactly what they want. 'Vaguely,' I should actually say. That's why I chose not to make theology my career. Commentary is not my thing.”

He patted the bundle of papers I was carrying on my shoulder.

“For Master Lefèvre, what's in here is clear as black and white. For him, Scripture has only one meaning: the christological. In other words, you try to read from it what Christ actually said — to restore its true religious substance, and to free it from the accumulated weight of more than a millennium of accumulated debris that has made it so hard to understand.”

He glanced at me with the hint of a smile.

“Did you ever feel drawn to entering a religious order?”

“Me? No — that was never a question for me.”

“It's true, you don't seem to have a mystical temperament. That's the difference between you and me. At fifteen I wanted to become a priest; I am a cleric and received the same training as the doctors of the Sorbonne — though mine was from monks who are certainly every bit as learned as those who trained those gentlemen who think so highly of themselves. I quickly realized that the whole thing, in this form, was absurd. That's why I chose to apprentice with a goldsmith. But perhaps I should never have switched to the printing trade — certain texts bother me too much.”

“But you are devout?”

“Of course! I believe in a purer, simpler form of prayer. I believe that the servants of the Church must set an example in piety and self-denial — that they should do good instead of enriching themselves at any cost. But what do I see, wherever I look? Armies of churchmen doing the exact opposite: giving themselves over to vice, forgetting God, and thinking a brief prayer is enough for God to forgive their worst outrages. They live as parasites off the people and off their simple faith. I'm not talking about morality here — you can witness that I rarely speak to you in terms of right and wrong. But you can't ask me not to see what's staring me in the face.”

All of this was still happening before the wars of religion — before Martin Luther had appeared on the scene. It was a time when Lefèvre d'Étaples was not yet seen as an enemy of the Sorbonne and the Church, and was saved from the stake — which he would otherwise have reached ten times over — only by the protection of the king and especially his sister, the future Queen of Navarre. I often think back on this conversation — or rather, on Master Antoine's monologue. There was nothing Protestant about it, since the Protestants didn't yet exist, but it's fair to say that he expressed, almost word for word, the very ideas that a few years later would bring fire and blood down on the kingdom.

While I was at Master Lefèvre d'Étaples's — either handing over proofs to Master Antoine or passing them to the young man I took to be the master's secretary — I tried to follow what was being said.

Even back then, Master Lefèvre struck me as an old man with snow-white hair, his gaze radiating from sky-blue eyes with the brightness of a child's. He was one of those rare men who wore no beard. I was told later that he had lost it in a fire. It had left no mark on his face; the beard had simply never grown back.

“I spent my childhood in a world where everything was still written by hand,” he explained to me between two rounds of corrections. “It was a world in which nothing ever seemed to change. But since I've been an adult, that world has grown — doubled in size, in fact. There's even talk that the Earth might not be as flat and as narrowly bounded as everyone always said. There was mention of a Polish monk, a certain Copernicus... And as if all that weren't enough, people have found ways to spread ideas at lightning speed.”

Personally, it still seemed to me that printing a book required a great deal of work — though I understood it was much faster than the old way, when books were copied by hand, one by one.

“Look,” Master Lefèvre continued, speaking more to Master Antoine than to me, “what you are doing here is very good. Now that the Word can be spread, it must be purified — which we are doing — but it must also become easier to read. The signs of the times announce that a renewal is coming.”

After that, the two masters seemed to forget I was there entirely. Instead of correcting texts, they began sketching letterforms. This gave me the chance to observe with some astonishment that Master Antoine — whom I saw all day at nothing but his printing press, and whose work maintaining type I had never stopped to think about — handled a pen like an artist.

“This is how Petrarch wrote his lowercase a,“ said Master Lefèvre. “I've seen his manuscripts in Italy. The humanist hand is a clear script. And the decision to use the Roman alphabet for the capital letters is excellent.”

“Did you know that in Poitiers I spent entire Sundays copying Roman inscriptions? They're found all over the city.”

With swift strokes he sketched letterforms on paper: MISET... BALI... RIBITORIAD...

“That was on a broken stone — the rest was missing. I was particularly drawn to those proportions. At the time, though, I had never heard of humanist lettering. The most I knew of was typography.”

Master Lefèvre studied the letters admiringly, first up close, then from farther back.

“And now they're sending you to Venice. I think that's an excellent idea.”

“Exclusively for the purpose of tracking down texts we can publish.”

“Take full advantage of the opportunity and learn everything Venice has to offer. Then bring back to France what our countryman Nicolas Jenson — who left Paris to learn the printing trade and never returned — left there.” He suddenly remembered I was in the room. “You're going to Venice too, it seems. Don't forget this, my children: the way a typeface sits on the page — whether it reads easily or not — is just as important as the purity of the text itself.”

He searched through his papers.

“Let the farmer sing it at his plow, let the weaver hum it to the rhythm of his shuttle, let the traveler ease the road with its stories; and may these become a topic of conversation for all Christians. Beautiful, isn't it? My friend Erasmus of Rotterdam wrote that to me. The lesson applies equally to the form of letters.”

“You should tell that to Master Estienne,” Master Antoine remarked. “I don't think he's fully grasped it.”

“The fact that he's sending you to Venice proves he understands enough.”

We went back to the proofreading. I'll admit I wasn't entirely focused. What had been said — the weight Master Lefèvre gave to typography — had thrown me off balance. The trade I was in the process of learning appeared to me suddenly, and for the first time, not as a craft, but as an adventure of the mind.
