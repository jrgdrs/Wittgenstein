Claude Garamont
Travel to Neuchâtel

It had turned cold again. During the day it had been warm, almost like a summer‘s day. But toward evening the sky had clouded over and it had begun to rain. Heavy showers at first, then a fine, steady drizzle. Just before sunset the clouds had parted for a moment, but the rain hadn‘t stopped. Everything was suddenly bathed in a rosy light, the surface of the lake glassy smooth. Had he not stepped out of his workshop at precisely that moment, he would have missed it entirely.

He climbed toward the Collegiate Church with his page proofs tucked under his arm. Normally he would have had his intern carry them, but this text was something special. He wanted to discuss a passage with Pastor Marcourt. Translating the Bible was a dangerous undertaking in certain parts of the world. Here, in Neuchâtel, it was merely extraordinarily difficult — all the more so because great men had tackled the task before him, and he couldn‘t yet be sure he could do it better.

He knocked at the door of the rectory. Madame Marcourt herself came to answer, a small child clinging to her skirt. He was struck every time by the youth of this woman at the side of a husband who seemed so much older, probably older than he was himself. Arranged marriages weren‘t uncommon among the wealthy, but that wasn‘t the case here. Marguerite de Crane had married Antoine Marcourt of her own free will.

“He‘s not here,” she said, almost before she‘d registered the man at her door, giving him no chance to speak. Another surprise. She was as lively as Marcourt was measured and dignified — except when he preached, when he became fire and fury — and seen in that light, he was a perfectly worthy husband for this sharp, spirited woman.

“I‘ll leave the proofs with you. Please tell him to look over the marked passages — I have a feeling we‘re not in line with the original text.”

“I‘ll let him know.”

On his way back he stopped by the customs office to check — you never knew — whether the reams of paper he‘d ordered had arrived and been held there. Nothing. If the paper supplier didn‘t get a move on, they‘d soon have nothing left to work with.

It was nearly dark by the time he reached the print shop; flickering candlelight showed through the shuttered windows.

He heard the sound of hooves. And then suddenly a figure emerged from the shadows, wrapped in a long coat, hood pulled down over his eyes, leading his horse by the reins. When he saw him, he stopped abruptly.

“Good evening, sir,” came a voice from the darkness.

An unfamiliar accent. Not from Neuchâtel, not from Geneva. The stranger was French.

“Good evening to you as well.”

“I‘m looking for Pierre de Vingle‘s print shop — I was told it was here.”

“Well, you‘ve found both the print shop and Pierre de Vingle. What can I do for you?”

“My name is Claude Garamont. I‘m a master printer, and I‘ve come from Paris to see you.”

“Then come in, sir. Let‘s not stand talking in the pouring rain.”

He pushed the door open. In the light he saw the stranger‘s fine-boned face beneath his beard, and his lean, compact frame. Probably in his thirties. He waited while Claude Garamont tied up his horse and set down his travel bag, then showed him inside.

His workshop was small: a typesetting case, an inking stone, a press, one apprentice, and himself — assisted by a handful of scholars who, like him, had fled to this region during the persecutions, and who helped prepare the texts.

Claude Garamont removed his coat. He was perhaps not as young as he‘d seemed at first glance: at the corners of his eyes were the fine lines that come from either experience or pain. All told, the visitor had to be around forty, even though his thick brown hair had no more grey in it than his neatly trimmed beard. He was not tall, one would say, but full of energy, resolve, strength, and barely contained anger.

He invited him in and asked him to sit, then told the housekeeper to prepare the room where Protestant visitors regularly stayed when they came to have a text printed — texts that would have been banned in Catholic countries. Since his own flight from Lyon, there had been so many of them that he was beginning to wonder whether it wasn‘t time to expand the workshop. He could have bet that the stranger wanted nothing different from him — and would no doubt also want to operate the press himself.

The housekeeper returned and set before the guest the remains of the stew the family had eaten earlier that evening.

“Please, help yourself, sir — we‘ve heated it up properly. It‘ll do you good. The weather is still dreadful, though it seems to be thinking about improving. It‘s so cold you wouldn‘t send a dog out at this hour.”

The man from Paris smiled for the first time since his arrival.

“Thank you, you‘re very kind. It‘s true — I‘m frozen to the bone and half-starved. You‘re saving my life.”

After that, he stayed fairly buttoned up. Where he could, he answered questions with single words. And when that wasn‘t possible, he kept his responses to the bare essentials. He‘d been running his own shop for some time. In Paris. His passion was designing and casting typefaces. Roman typefaces, he added in response to a follow-up question. He‘d been travelling for several days. Before that he‘d spent several months in Basel. After the meal, Claude Garamont looked directly at Pierre de Vingle for the first time. He had light brown eyes, with something green in them — cat‘s eyes.

“If you‘ll allow me, sir, I‘ll explain my reason for coming tomorrow. I‘m exhausted, and the matter is complicated.”

You can‘t refuse a guest‘s request. Pierre de Vingle was curious, but he yielded.

He was not kept in suspense for long. The following morning the stranger came downstairs at the same time as he did. After breakfast he accompanied Master de Vingle into the print shop, where he looked everything over. He leaned over the type case, took out a character, then another, and another, carried them outside to examine them in the daylight. The eye of a type cutter, Pierre de Vingle told himself.

Finally the two men withdrew to the proofreading room, which was empty at that hour.

Claude Garamont reached into the inner pocket of his jacket and produced a sheet of paper, which he carefully unfolded, smoothed flat, and laid before Pierre.

“It may seem absurd to have made such a journey on the basis of a rumour, but I‘ve come to ask you whether it‘s true that you printed this poster. Judging by the type I just examined, I‘d say: yes.”

Pierre de Vingle immediately understood what this was about. To keep his composure, he picked up the sheet and began reading aloud:

“Articles veracious concerning the horrible, great and insufferable abuses of the Papal Mass, set up in direct opposition to the Holy Supper of Our Lord Jesus Christ. I call upon heaven and earth to bear witness to the truth against this pompous and arrogant Popish Mass, by which the world — if God does not soon provide remedy — shall be utterly ruined, corrupted, lost and forsaken; since Our Lord is thereby blasphemed in so terrible a manner, and the people led astray and blinded: which can and must no longer be endured nor permitted to continue.”

Then his gaze moved further down the page, slowing as he reached the end, and he read the conclusion aloud.

“...with this Mass they have seized everything, destroyed everything, devoured everything; they have disinherited princes and kings, merchants, lords, and all — one may say — that is dead or alive. Thanks to it they live without care, they need do nothing, let alone study — what more could one want? There is thus certainly no cause for rejoicing in their maintaining it; they conduct themselves like marauders, they kill, they burn, they destroy, they murder all who contradict them, for they know only force. Truth is foreign to them. Truth threatens them. Truth pursues and hunts them. They are terrified of truth. By it they shall soon be destroyed. Fiat, Fiat, Amen.”

He looked up. Claude Garamont‘s eyes had gone almost black, his face rigid, lips pressed tight, his pallor frightening.

“Well? Were you the one with the brilliant idea of calling the King a bloody villain?”

Careful.

“It‘s possible this manifesto came from my print shop, esteemed colleague, but it must have been some time ago — it reminds me of what our pastor says. Pastor Marcourt.”

“You didn‘t write it!”

He laughed.

“Good God, no. I‘d be incapable of writing something like that. It really must have been Pastor Marcourt.”

Garamont began to think this through, weighing the information; eventually he conceded.

“I see. It‘s true that my informant was a printer, not a theologian. He told me you had been persecuted, had been forced to leave behind your wife and children and flee the kingdom — and that you were the author of this text.”

“Upon my honour, no. I‘m quite sceptical of the Marian cult, I admit that, but I would never proceed with such ferocity. My methods are different, and I‘m opposed to any provocation. I prefer the principle of live and let live. May I ask why you‘re interested in the origins of this poster?”

“Since you are neither a theologian nor the author of this text, you may perhaps not know that it was posted everywhere — from Paris to Amboise, and right up to the door of the King‘s bedchamber — that it resulted in the deaths of an unknown number of people, at least fifty, and achieved the exact opposite of what the author intended. Instead of awakening souls, it unleashed an unprecedented persecution and alienated the Protestants from those people who, only the evening before, would have been willing to sit down and talk with them.”

Pierre de Vingle knew all of this well enough, but preferred to stay vague.

“That the situation has become extremely serious in recent months for those who are not in full agreement with the Church of Rome — that much I know. We feel the effects of it here directly, every time something happens: people come looking for refuge in the Neuchâtel region, in the Vaud, in Geneva; and even those who want, for whatever reason, to go to Basel or Berne pass through here and tell their stories.”

“And it wasn‘t clear to you that the latest wave was triggered by that?” Claude Garamont slammed his hand hard on the poster. Pierre de Vingle sidestepped the question.

“Various scholars, Pierre Olivétan foremost among them, are preparing a translation of the Bible, and I‘m aware that this could cause a storm.”

The two men stared at each other in silence for what felt like half an eternity.

“And what brought you here, Master Garamont?”

Claude Garamont found it hard to speak; you could see from his throat how tightly he was wound.

“The man accused of having printed this poster was my master. He taught me my trade and treated me like a son. Later he became my mother‘s husband.”

Silence. When he tried to continue, Claude Garamont could barely get the words out — tears were strangling his voice. “I swore before God to track down the man who wrote this deranged text and spread it among the people without giving a moment‘s thought to the serious consequences it might bring. I swore it above all for my poor mother — or rather, for what is left of the woman who lived only for her husband. And I swore it for myself: to make sure he has to swallow this.”

Pierre de Vingle smiled despite himself.

“If this pamphlet is Master Marcourt‘s work, you‘ll quickly understand that he is not the sort of man you can make swallow what he‘s written.”

“The screed was launched from this house without any consideration for the consequences those who would have to bear them. The King was on the verge of establishing a kind of religious peace by neutralising the Sorbonne. But His Majesty is a man of the moment, and these posters destroyed the moment. His Majesty was so enraged that the pendulum swung entirely the other way. It won‘t take much more before His Majesty is even more bigoted than the fanatics at the Sorbonne, who are currently doing as they please with him. In any case, His Majesty is now giving them free rein, whereas before he had kept them in check. The result: Master Antoine Augereau and probably fifty others are dead. The Sorbonne had only been waiting for a pretext to move against those who had fallen into disrepute at the Faculty of Theology. Your Marcourt gave them that pretext, and in doing so dealt a lethal blow to the very people who stood for his own cause. Those who had been on the verge of winning Protestants over to their ideas recoiled from them in horror.”

“I am deeply grieved — truly grieved. But Master Marcourt will hardly be shaken by that. He‘s a fanatic in his own way. I‘ll take you to him and you can speak with him. But he has made Martin Luther‘s cause his own, and he is convinced that every sacrifice must be made for it. He has done so himself — he left Lyon, where he had lived, and arrived here with nothing but the clothes on his back. Thanks to his eloquence he became our chief pastor.”

“Do you like him?”

Pierre de Vingle had to think about the question. At worst, the newcomer was a spy for the Sorbonne.

“Between us, I find him too demanding, too lacking in warmth — unlike Master Farel, for instance, who has always stayed close to ordinary people and whose voice never pitches up into a frenzy. Or Master Olivétan, who is gentleness itself. But I assume Marcourt stands for a just cause.”

He tried to get a read on Garamont.

“Are you Lutheran or Zwinglian?”

“I have no idea. I‘m a craftsman first and foremost. And like Master Augereau, a good Christian. I believe we ought to talk to one another. Hardline Catholics are insufferable — they wanted to go so far as to have printing banned, and the King is siding with them.”

“What? Ban printing? But—”

“Your Marcourt gave them the idea, you should know. While His Majesty was still furious about the posters against the Mass, someone arranged to circulate A Brief, Very Useful and Salutary Treatise on the Holy Eucharist…”

“Yes — I printed that myself, personally.”

“The only positive thing about that text is the fact that the author accuses himself of having written and printed the posters against the Mass. And because Master Augereau is dead and therefore could not possibly have been responsible, His Majesty was even further enraged. The King had had his sister‘s favourite printer executed — a man who had always protested his innocence. And it turned out that he had indeed been wrongly accused. Master Beda, who had pushed the King to carry out Master Augereau‘s execution, paid dearly for it. He was forced to make a public apology and was expelled from the city — he barely escaped the stake himself. Master Augereau‘s friends and those of the Chevalier de Berquin applauded, but only half-heartedly, since neither the two martyrs nor the others were brought back to life by it. And until the matter is resolved, they are shutting down the print shops.”

A heavy sigh.

“That won‘t last, of course. The only useful thing about this ban is that it gave me the time to come and visit you. I‘m confident that by the time I return we‘ll be able to use our presses again.”

He smiled without warmth.

“Hardline Protestants are just as repugnant to me as bigoted Romans. For the same reason: fall into an extreme — which is easier than thinking — and you become foolish. The follies differ, but they are follies all the same. Every last one of them. And they are dangerous. People must talk to one another rather than kill each other. Otherwise humanity stays on the floor, forever.”

Pierre de Vingle looked at him for a long moment without speaking. This stranger was saying aloud what he himself thought in the deepest part of himself, but had never put into words.

“We are craftsmen, not thinkers. Let us leave the theory to the Doctors of Theology, who are called to that task.”

“Those were Master Augereau‘s exact words. He ended up on the gallows all the same.”

“And now?”

“Let us go to Master — Marcourt, you said.”

“Yes. Antoine Marcourt. He comes from Lyon, though like me he‘s originally from Picardy.”

“Wherever he comes from, I want to make his acquaintance.” A smile flickered across his lips. “One thing is certain — even if I made him swallow that damned poster, my master would not come back to life.” He thought for a moment. “But first, I‘d like you to read the memoir I‘ve written about my relationship with Master Antoine and the events that led to his death.”

He rose, went up to his room, and came back with a bundle of papers held together by a leather strap.

“Here is my story. At night I typeset what I wrote during the day. Since Master Augereau‘s death I haven‘t been able to sleep.”

Pierre de Vingle took the pages.

“It seems to me that you didn‘t come here to punish Master Marcourt at all, but for your own sake — to find peace of mind after confronting him. It will help you in your grief.”

“I wish you were right.”

“May I offer a suggestion?”

“Please, Master de Vingle.”

“Give me time to read your memoir. A few days. I‘m happy to put you up here. You can observe Master Marcourt from a distance and form your own impression of him — and I‘ll form mine of your text, which you can then hand to him.”

Garamont narrowed his eyes and furrowed his brow.

“Agreed,” he said at last. “In the meantime I can lend a hand in your print shop. That way I‘ll attract the least attention.”

They shook hands.

Pierre de Vingle opened the bundle. The text was set in Roman type of extraordinary purity, delicacy and beauty. Until now Pierre had always refused to use Roman letterforms, believing them to be far less legible than was commonly claimed. But these…

“Where does the typeface come from — the one you used to set your memoir?”

Claude Garamont‘s voice trembled again as he answered.

“It is the work of Master Antoine Augereau. God rest his soul — he was a great scholar and the finest type-cutter for Roman faces in the Kingdom of France and Navarre. Perhaps in the whole world.”

“Antoine Augereau... I think I‘ve heard that name. Perhaps through my father-in-law in Lyon... He was a printer.”

“What was his name?”

“Master Claude Nourry. At the sign of the Prince.”

“The printer of Pantagruel?”

“The very same. He‘s passed away now — peace to his memory.”

“And you? How did you come to be here?”

“I had printed Master Lefèvre‘s French Bible translation once too often. I took precautions, but to no avail. The day came when I had to disappear.”

Silence.

“And you, Master Garamont?”

“I am not — or rather, I am no longer — a printer. I am a type-cutter. I learned everything from Master Augereau.”

Pierre de Vingle thought for a moment. If he let Claude Garamont work alongside him, he would — if the man was truly as skilled as his text suggested — have time to read.

“Then we have a deal, my friend. To work.”

The two men rose together and returned to the print shop.

It took the whole day for the Parisian printer to get settled and take up the work. After casting his first glance at the text he would be working on, he expressed surprise.

“But... This is yet again the Bible in Master Lefèvre‘s translation. Are you reprinting it?”

“No. If you look carefully, you‘ll see it‘s a revised version — the work of Master Olivétan. He takes Master Lefèvre‘s French text as his starting point, certainly, but he compares it against the ancient texts, Greek and even Hebrew. Master Pierre is a great scholar. He uses the Lefèvre text only where it agrees with the sources he believes to be the original ones — texts that Master d‘Étaples had no access to.”

He smiled slyly.

“Pierre Olivétan may be a great scholar, but he is still a young man. And as you know, young people tend to want to reinvent the wheel without attending to what is essential — they would never have thought of the idea had it not already existed. But one must let them learn through experience. And if it is all to the greater glory of the Creator…”

Claude Garamont agreed.

“Well said.”

He set to work.

Master Marcourt had not appeared — he was perhaps in Geneva. In the meantime, Master de Vingle resolved to make use of the free hours and read.

He immersed himself in Claude Garamont‘s memoir.
